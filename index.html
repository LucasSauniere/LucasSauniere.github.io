02 · Mission

Euclid, at Lagrange 2.

ESA's Euclid space telescope maps a third of the sky — roughly 1.5 billion galaxies over six years — from the gravitational calm of Lagrange point 2, 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. Its mission: to chart the geometry of the dark Universe.

03 · Optics

From photon to PSF.

A star is, for all practical purposes, a point. But no telescope records a point. Light passes through a circular aperture and diffracts; whatever aberrations live in the optics imprint themselves on the wavefront; what lands on the detector is a smeared-out fingerprint called the point spread function.

Every source in every image is this shape, scaled by brightness. Recover the PSF and you recover the truth behind the blur.

04 · The problem

An ideal telescope doesn't exist.

If Euclid were diffraction-limited, the PSF would be a crisp Airy pattern — the mathematical ideal. In practice, thermal gradients flex the optical bench, the primary mirror settles by nanometres after launch, and the spacecraft itself breathes as it orbits the Sun. Each effect smears the PSF in a different direction.

A millimetre of astigmatism at the mirror becomes a galaxy-scale ellipticity bias in the final catalogue — exactly the signal we're trying to measure. So we measure the blur instead.

05 · Imaging

Why we care.

Euclid's headline science is weak gravitational lensing: measuring the tiny ellipticities of distant galaxies to map the dark Universe. The cosmological signal is roughly a one-percent shear on each galaxy. The telescope's aberrated PSF imprints a few-percent shape bias of its own — same units, larger amplitude, wrong physics.

On the left, a synthetic patch of sky as it really is. On the right, the same patch after the as-built PSF has done its work: the field convolved with the kernel in the middle. Round ellipticals lean the way the PSF leans; small galaxies get merged; point sources blur into the kernel itself.

That bias has to be subtracted before any cosmology is done. To subtract it, we have to know the PSF — at every position on the focal plane, at every wavelength, at every time. The rest of the story is how.

06 · Measurement

The stars tell us the PSF.

Every exposure contains tens of thousands of stars. Stars are unresolved — their image is the PSF at that field position, modulo photon noise. By collecting stars across the focal plane we build a sparse but global map of the blur.

But the PSF varies: with position on the 6×6 CCD mosaic, with wavelength, with time, with the spacecraft's thermal state. No two stars share the same PSF exactly. The map has to interpolate, extrapolate, and denoise — all at once.

07 · Denoise

The raw stamps are awful.

The stars we use as PSF probes don't cooperate: most are faint, so the stamps are dominated by shot noise; and VIS samples the PSF below Nyquist, so even at high signal-to-noise the stamp on the detector is a coarse, pixelated rendering of the true continuous PSF. A polynomial fit to those stamps inherits both problems and bakes them into the model.

Our pipeline sidesteps that by using a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) as a learned prior over PSFs. Given a noisy, undersampled stamp, the DDPM runs the reverse diffusion process for T steps, peeling off variance at each step, until what remains is a clean PSF estimate consistent with everything the network has ever seen during training. Only then does the regressor see the stamp.

08 · The Network

From PSF stamp to Zernike coefficients.

Once the PSF is denoised, an Inception-style CNN regresses the Zernike coefficients that describe its wavefront. The first layer does the heavy lifting: a small kernel is dragged across the stamp, and at every position it sums the input pixels weighted by the kernel. The output is a feature map that responds where the input matches what the kernel is tuned to detect.

An Inception block runs several kernel sizes in parallel — 1×1, 3×3, 5×5 — and concatenates their feature maps, so the network can pick up both fine pixel-level structure and broader shape cues in the same forward pass. Stack a few of those blocks and a regression head, and the network maps a 32-pixel stamp to a handful of Zernike coefficients: defocus (Z₄), astigmatism (Z₅, Z₆), coma (Z₇, Z₈), spherical (Z₁₁).

09 · Validation

Does it work?

An unvalidated model is a rumour. To trust the network we need to see what it predicts on stars it has never seen, compare those predictions to truth, and check that the residuals are consistent with noise — not only on average, but across every corner of the focal plane.

On the right: three panels for a held-out star — truth, prediction, and residual. As training proceeds, the residual collapses toward the photon-noise floor. The map below shows fractional size error ΔT / T across the field. We want it featureless.

10 · Euclid

Two ramps, one minimum.

Commissioning used two M2 focus ramps. A coarse ramp walked ΔzM2 from −50 to +50 µm in steps of 10 µm, three exposures per step — wide enough to bracket the true best focus. A fine ramp then refined it from −10 to +10 µm in steps of 2 µm.

Because VIS undersamples the PSF, the model recovers only |Z₄|: positive and negative defocus leave near-identical stamps after pixelation. The scatter is therefore a V, not a line. Best focus sits at the vertex.

As you scroll, the coarse ramp builds its V; then the fine ramp fills in, tightening the vertex. The recovered best focus is reported against the engineering value both in absolute microns and normalised by the campaign's step size.
